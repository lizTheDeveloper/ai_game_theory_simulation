# Historical Carbon Sink Parameters: 1990 vs 2025

**Research Date:** November 26, 2025
**Researcher:** Cynthia (super-alignment-researcher)
**Context:** Climate hindcast validation (1990-2010) showing CO2 31% too high due to anachronistic sink parameters

## Executive Summary

Carbon sink strengths have increased substantially from 1990 to 2025, with ocean sinks growing ~50% (2.2→3.4 GtC/yr) and land sinks more than doubling (1.4→3.1 GtC/yr). The airborne fraction remained remarkably stable at ~0.44-0.45 despite debates about potential increases. Sink saturation is not well-defined as a single 0-1 scalar in the literature; instead, researchers focus on temporal trends in absolute uptake rates, regional saturation (e.g., Southern Ocean in 1990s), and chemical buffering capacity (Revelle factor).

## Key Findings

### 1. Ocean CO2 Absorption

**1990s Baseline:** 2.2 ± 0.4 GtC/yr (IPCC assessment, widely used for model calibration)

**2020s Values:**
- 2014-2023 average: 2.9 ± 0.4 GtC/yr (26% of total emissions)
- 2024: 3.4 ± 0.4 GtC/yr

**Temporal Trend:**
- **1990s:** Global stagnation at ~2.0 PgC/yr, particularly in Southern Ocean
- **2000s:** Strengthening to ~3.0 PgC/yr after 2000
- **2010s-2020s:** Continued growth to 2.9-3.4 GtC/yr
- Overall pattern: ~50-55% increase from 1990s to 2024

**Mechanisms:**
1. **Southern Ocean Dynamics:** Weakening in 1990s due to increased winds (Le Quéré et al. 2007), followed by recovery in 2000s
2. **Extra-tropical Strengthening:** Enhanced uptake outside tropics after 2000
3. **Chemical Saturation (Revelle Factor):** Ocean buffering capacity declining as DIC increases, but absolute uptake still growing due to rising atmospheric CO2 gradient

### 2. Land CO2 Absorption

**1990s Baseline:** 1.4 ± 0.7 GtC/yr (IPCC Third Assessment Report, Prentice et al.)
- Alternative estimate (Le Quéré et al. 2003): 1.2 ± 0.8 GtC/yr

**2020s Values:**
- 2010s average: 3.1 ± 0.6 GtC/yr (more than doubled since 1960s)
- 2014-2023 average: 3.2 ± 0.9 GtC/yr (31% of total emissions)
- 2024: 1.9 ± 1.1 GtC/yr (significant decline due to climate impacts)

**Temporal Trend:**
- **1980s:** -0.2 to -0.3 GtC/yr (negative = sink)
- **1990s:** -1.4 ± 0.7 GtC/yr (large increase from 1980s)
- **2000s:** ~1.8 GtC/yr
- **2010s:** 3.1 ± 0.6 GtC/yr (peak)
- **2024:** 1.9 ± 1.1 GtC/yr (climate-driven weakening)
- Overall pattern: +121% increase from 1990s to 2010s peak, but recent weakening

**Mechanisms:**
1. **CO2 Fertilization:** Enhanced photosynthesis from rising atmospheric CO2
2. **Forest Regrowth:** Abandoned agricultural land in temperate regions (particularly Northern Hemisphere)
3. **Tropical Forest Sink:** Intact tropical forests sequestered ~50% of global terrestrial uptake in 1990s-early 2000s
4. **Climate-Driven Weakening (2020s):** Extreme heat, drought, and fires reducing sink efficiency
5. **Regional Saturation:** Eastern Europe sink declined 2010-2019 as regrowth forests matured

### 3. Sink Saturation Concept

**Critical Finding:** The literature does NOT use a simple 0-1 "sink saturation" scalar. Instead, saturation is characterized by:

**A. Temporal Variability in Absolute Uptake:**
- Sinks have grown in absolute terms (GtC/yr) despite rising emissions
- But growth rate slowed: ocean sink stagnant 2016-2023 after rapid 2002-2016 growth
- Land sink efficiency varies decadally based on climate, land use, volcanic eruptions

**B. Regional Saturation Examples:**
- **Southern Ocean 1990s:** Sink weakened ~0.08 PgC/yr/decade due to wind changes (25% efficiency reduction)
- **Eastern Europe 2010s:** Sink declined as forest regrowth saturated
- **Tropical Forests:** Some evidence of uptake decline in mature forests (Lewis et al.)

**C. Chemical Buffering Capacity (Ocean):**
- **Revelle Factor:** Measures resistance to CO2 uptake (typically 9-15, higher = less uptake per pCO2 change)
- **Trend:** Increasing over time as DIC accumulates (13% decline in absorption capacity since 1992)
- **Mechanism:** As ocean absorbs CO2, pH drops and carbonate chemistry becomes less buffered

**Approximating "Sink Saturation" for Simulation:**

If we MUST define a 0-1 saturation parameter, the most defensible approach is:

```
Saturation = f(cumulative_emissions, baseline_capacity)

1990: ~0.10-0.15  (1,000 GtCO2 cumulative / ~10,000 GtCO2 baseline capacity)
2025: ~0.24-0.30  (2,400 GtCO2 cumulative / ~10,000 GtCO2 baseline capacity)
```

**However, this is a SIMPLIFICATION.** The real system shows:
- Sinks growing in absolute terms (not declining)
- But fractional uptake of emissions declining slightly (airborne fraction stable-to-rising)
- Regional heterogeneity (some sinks saturating, others strengthening)

**Recommendation:** Model sink strength as time-dependent functions calibrated to observed GtC/yr values, NOT as capacity * (1 - saturation). The current code structure conflates capacity reduction with saturation, which is not how the literature models this.

### 4. Airborne Fraction (Validation Metric)

**Definition:** Fraction of annual anthropogenic CO2 emissions remaining in atmosphere

**Historical Values:**
- **1960-2010:** 0.44-0.45 (remarkably stable)
- **1990s:** ~0.44 ± 0.06
- **2000s:** ~0.44-0.45
- **2010s:** ~0.45 (slight increase debated)
- **2014-2023:** 0.47 (5.2 ± 0.02 GtC/yr atmospheric growth / 11.1 GtC/yr emissions)

**Key Debates:**
- **Canadell et al. (2007), Raupach et al. (2008):** Found evidence of increasing airborne fraction (~0.3%/yr trend)
- **Knorr (2009):** Incorporating uncertainties, increasing trend NOT statistically significant
- **Consensus (Raupach et al. 2014, Bennedsen et al. 2019):** Airborne fraction stable at ~0.44

**For 1990-2010 Hindcast Validation:**
- Target airborne fraction: **0.44-0.45**
- If simulation shows CO2 31% too high (549 vs 390 ppm in 2010), likely causes:
  1. Sinks too weak (using 2025 saturation for 1990)
  2. Emissions too high (but GCP data applied, so less likely)
  3. Initial CO2 (1990) wrong
  4. Sink temporal evolution not captured

### Recommended Temporal Evolution (1990-2025)

**Option 1: Linear Interpolation (Simplest)**
```typescript
function getSinkParameters(year: number) {
  const t = (year - 1990) / (2025 - 1990);  // 0 in 1990, 1 in 2025

  return {
    oceanGtC: 2.2 + t * (3.4 - 2.2),  // 2.2 → 3.4
    landGtC: 1.4 + t * (1.9 - 1.4),   // 1.4 → 1.9
    saturation: 0.12 + t * (0.28 - 0.12)  // 0.12 → 0.28
  };
}
```

**Option 2: Piecewise (More Accurate to Literature)**
```typescript
function getSinkParameters(year: number) {
  if (year < 2000) {
    // 1990s: stagnant ocean, growing land
    return {
      oceanGtC: 2.2,
      landGtC: 1.4,
      saturation: 0.12 + (year - 1990) * 0.004  // Slow increase
    };
  } else if (year < 2010) {
    // 2000s: both sinks strengthening
    return {
      oceanGtC: 2.2 + (year - 2000) * 0.05,  // +0.05/yr
      landGtC: 1.4 + (year - 2000) * 0.04,   // +0.04/yr
      saturation: 0.16 + (year - 2000) * 0.006
    };
  } else if (year < 2020) {
    // 2010s: land sink peaks
    return {
      oceanGtC: 2.7 + (year - 2010) * 0.02,  // Slower growth
      landGtC: 1.8 + (year - 2010) * 0.13,   // Rapid to 3.1
      saturation: 0.22 + (year - 2010) * 0.008
    };
  } else {
    // 2020s: land sink weakens due to climate impacts
    return {
      oceanGtC: 2.9 + (year - 2020) * 0.1,   // Recovering
      landGtC: 3.1 - (year - 2020) * 0.24,   // Declining to 1.9
      saturation: 0.30  // Near-constant recent
    };
  }
}
```

**Option 3: Rethink Saturation (Recommended)**

The current `sinkCapacity = (ocean + land) * (1 - saturation)` formulation is problematic because:
1. Observed sinks are GROWING absolutely, not declining
2. Saturation affects fractional efficiency, not total capacity
3. Regional heterogeneity matters (Southern Ocean ≠ North Atlantic)

**Better approach:**
```typescript
// Directly use observed sink rates (no saturation scalar)
function getCarbonSinks(state: GameState): { oceanGtC: number, landGtC: number } {
  const year = 1990 + state.currentMonth / 12;

  // Base sinks from empirical data (GtC/yr)
  let oceanGtC = interpolate(year, OCEAN_SINK_TIMESERIES);
  let landGtC = interpolate(year, LAND_SINK_TIMESERIES);

  // Apply climate feedbacks (temperature, pH, drought)
  oceanGtC *= getOceanEfficiencyModifier(state);
  landGtC *= getLandEfficiencyModifier(state);

  return { oceanGtC, landGtC };
}

function getOceanEfficiencyModifier(state: GameState): number {
  // Warmer SST reduces solubility
  const tempEffect = Math.exp(-0.0423 * state.climateState.temperatureAnomaly);

  // Lower pH reduces buffering (Revelle factor increase)
  const pHEffect = (state.oceanHealth.pH - 7.8) / (8.1 - 7.8);  // 1.0 at pH 8.1, 0.0 at pH 7.8

  return tempEffect * pHEffect;
}

function getLandEfficiencyModifier(state: GameState): number {
  // Extreme heat reduces photosynthesis
  const heatStress = state.climateState.temperatureAnomaly > 1.5
    ? Math.exp(-0.2 * (state.climateState.temperatureAnomaly - 1.5))
    : 1.0;

  // Deforestation reduces sink
  const forestEffect = state.ecology.forestCover / FOREST_COVER_1990;

  return heatStress * forestEffect;
}
```

## Uncertainties and Limitations

### 1. Large Uncertainty Ranges
- Land sink 1990s: ±0.7 GtC (±50% relative uncertainty)
- Land sink 2024: ±1.1 GtC (±58% relative uncertainty)
- Ocean sink: ±0.4 GtC (±18-20% relative uncertainty)

**Implication:** Hindcast validation should allow ±15-20% error in CO2 trajectory

### 2. Sink Saturation Not Well-Defined
- No agreed 0-1 scalar in literature
- Regional and temporal heterogeneity dominant
- Chemical vs biological vs physical saturation differ

**Implication:** Model sink evolution as empirical time series + climate feedbacks, not as simple saturation parameter

### 3. Recent Climate-Driven Weakening
- 2023-2024 land sink dropped from 3.1 to 1.9 GtC/yr
- Extreme heat, drought, fires driving decline
- Unclear if temporary fluctuation or long-term trend

**Implication:** 2020s values may not be stable baseline for future projections

### 4. Revelle Factor Complexity
- Varies regionally (9 in tropics, 15 in Southern Ocean)
- Increases with DIC but also affected by temperature, alkalinity
- 13% decline in capacity since 1992, but absolute uptake still growing

**Implication:** Simple saturation parameter can't capture ocean chemistry evolution

### 5. Land Use Change Emissions
- Deforestation reduces sink (lost forest area)
- But also counted as emissions (biomass burning, decay)
- Reforestation/afforestation increases sink
- Separation between ELUC and SLAND involves modeling assumptions

**Implication:** Land sink uncertainty includes definitional ambiguity
