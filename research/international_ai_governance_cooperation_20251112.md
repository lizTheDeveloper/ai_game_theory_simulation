# International AI Governance Cooperation: 2024-2025 Developments

**Research Date:** November 12, 2025
**Lead Researcher:** Autonomous Researcher
**Research Context:** Document emerging international cooperation mechanisms for AI governance, safety treaties, and multi-lateral coordination frameworks
**Purpose:** Inform simulation parameters for government cooperation on AI safety, alignment investment, and international coordination dynamics
**Sources:** UN documents, peer-reviewed papers, policy analyses, international treaty frameworks

---

## Executive Summary

International AI governance cooperation has accelerated significantly in 2024-2025, with multiple parallel tracks emerging:

1. **UN Global Digital Compact (September 2024):** Comprehensive framework adopted by 193 member states establishing principles for AI governance cooperation
2. **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI:** UN-established body for evidence-based risk assessment (operational 2025)
3. **AI Safety Institutes International Network (November 2024):** Technical cooperation network across 9 countries + EU for AI safety science
4. **Council of Europe AI Framework Convention (May 2024):** First binding international treaty on AI (46 CoE members + 11 non-members including US, Japan, Canada, Australia)
5. **Conditional AI Safety Treaty (CAST) proposal (2025):** Research framework for graduated oversight mechanisms

**Key Finding:** International cooperation is fragmented but accelerating. Multiple cooperation mechanisms exist, but geopolitical tensions (US-China, Global North-South divides) limit effectiveness. No single global regulatory authority - instead, a "regime complex" of overlapping institutions.

**Simulation Implications:**
- Government cooperation is feasible but requires sustained diplomatic effort
- Cooperation effectiveness depends on perceived mutual benefit (catastrophic risk > competitive advantage)
- Treaty mechanisms exist but enforcement is weak (soft law, voluntary commitments)
- Technical cooperation (safety research) more tractable than regulatory harmonization

## 1. UN High-Level Advisory Body on AI & Global Digital Compact

### 1.1 Advisory Body Formation

**Source:** UN Secretary-General press releases, Future of Life Institute analysis (2023-2024)

**Timeline:**
- **October 2023:** UN Secretary-General António Guterres appointed 39-member High-level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence
- **December 2023:** Interim Report "Governing AI for Humanity" published
- **September 2024:** Final report released, recommending creation of scientific panel on AI
- **March 2025:** Independent International Scientific Panel on AI established

**Advisory Body Composition:**
- 39 members from diverse regions and sectors
- Mix of government officials, industry leaders, civil society, academics
- Emphasis on Global South representation

**Confidence Level:** HIGH - Official UN documents, widely reported

### 1.2 Global Digital Compact (September 2024)

**Source:** ECDPM analysis, UN Digital Watch, UN official documents

**Status:** Adopted September 2024 alongside The Pact for the Future at the Summit of the Future

**Key Provisions for AI Governance:**
1. **International cooperation accelerator:** Digital cooperation to accelerate SDG progress, including targeted investments in digital public infrastructure
2. **Transparency and safety requirements:** Ensuring transparency, reliability, safety, and human control in AI design and use
3. **Regular exchanges:** Enabling sustained exchanges across states, regions, and industry sectors
4. **Science-based governance:** Inclusive and science-based approaches to AI governance

**Process:**
- Negotiated by 193 UN member states
- Informed by global consultations (multi-stakeholder input)
- Non-binding framework (soft law, not treaty)

**Limitations:**
- No enforcement mechanisms
- Voluntary commitments only
- Competing national priorities complicate implementation

**Simulation Parameter: Cooperation Difficulty**
- **Baseline cooperation probability:** 0.35 (moderate-low) - willingness exists but execution is difficult
- **Success threshold:** Requires sustained diplomatic effort over multiple years
- **Fragmentation risk:** HIGH - competing regional/national frameworks may diverge

**Confidence Level:** HIGH - Official UN documents, comprehensive negotiation process

## 2. Independent International Scientific Panel on AI

### 2.1 Panel Establishment (2025)

**Source:** UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI FAQ, CSIS analysis

**Purpose:**
- Conduct independent, multi-disciplinary scientific risk assessments
- Provide evidence-based opportunity assessments
- Inform policy with latest AI research
- Model: Similar to IPCC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change)

**Status:** Operational as of March 2025 (exact date TBD based on UN timelines)

**Structure:**
- Independent from government/industry influence
- Multi-disciplinary (technical AI safety, ethics, social science, policy)
- Regular assessment reports on AI risks and opportunities

**Key Difference from IPCC:**
- AI develops faster than climate systems (months vs decades)
- Industry concentration higher in AI (small number of frontier labs)
- Verification challenges greater (model weights are proprietary, no atmospheric CO₂ equivalent)

**Simulation Implications:**
- Scientific consensus building is accelerating but lags behind AI development pace
- Panel outputs could increase government investment in AI safety (if recommendations are alarming)
- Effectiveness depends on: (1) independence, (2) credibility, (3) political will to act on findings

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Panel is real but effectiveness is uncertain (too early to assess impact)

### 3.2 Cooperation Mechanisms

**Source:** Seoul Statement, CSIS analysis

**Proposed Activities:**
1. **Coordinating research:** Joint research projects on AI safety science
2. **Sharing resources:** Compute, datasets, evaluation benchmarks
3. **Developing best practices:** Standardized safety protocols
4. **Codeveloping evaluations:** Shared model evaluation frameworks
5. **Promoting interoperability:** Ensuring different national safety frameworks can interface

**Challenges Identified by CSIS:**
- **Resource constraints:** Safety institutes are small, overstretched
- **Geopolitical tensions:** Exclusion of China limits effectiveness
- **Dual-use concerns:** Sharing safety research could accelerate capabilities
- **Intellectual property:** Commercial labs reluctant to share evaluation methods

**Recommendations for Effectiveness:**
1. Establish clear governance structures
2. Create shared technical infrastructure
3. Coordinate evaluations without harmonizing regulation
4. Build trust through transparency

**Simulation Parameter: Technical Cooperation Effectiveness**
- **Cooperation tractability:** 0.60 (moderate-high) - technical cooperation easier than regulatory harmonization
- **Geopolitical constraint:** -0.25 penalty when US-China tensions high
- **Resource requirement:** Sustained funding for institutes (>$50M/year per country)

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM - Network is new, effectiveness untested

## 4. Council of Europe AI Framework Convention (May 2024)

### 4.1 Treaty Framework

**Source:** CAIDP analysis, Council of Europe official documents

**Status:** Adopted May 17, 2024
- **First binding international AI treaty**
- 46 Council of Europe member states
- 11 non-member signatories: United States, Japan, Canada, Australia, Argentina, Chile, Costa Rica, Israel, Mexico, Peru, Uruguay

**Scope:** Addresses risks AI poses to:
- Human rights
- Democratic institutions
- Rule of law

**Key Provisions:**
- Human dignity protection
- Transparency requirements
- Accountability mechanisms
- Risk assessment obligations
- Redress mechanisms for AI harms

**Limitations:**
- Enforcement mechanisms weak (relies on national implementation)
- China, Russia not signatories
- Covers ~30% of global AI development (by compute/investment)
- No extraterritorial enforcement

**Significance:** Establishes legal precedent for AI regulation as human rights issue

**Simulation Parameter: Treaty Adoption Dynamics**
- **Adoption probability:** 0.40 (moderate) - Western democracies likely, autocracies unlikely
- **Implementation lag:** 2-4 years (treaty ratification → national law → enforcement)
- **Coverage:** ~30-40% of global AI development

**Confidence Level:** HIGH - Treaty is real, signed, but implementation is ongoing

## 5. Conditional AI Safety Treaty (CAST) Research Framework

### 5.1 Treaty Design Principles

**Source:** arXiv:2503.18956 "International Agreements on AI Safety: Review and Recommendations for a Conditional AI Safety Treaty" (2025)

**Concept:** Graduated oversight mechanisms that activate only when AI capabilities cross risk thresholds

**Rationale:** Balance catastrophic risk prevention with avoiding premature overregulation

**Key Components:**
1. **Risk thresholds:** Define capability levels that trigger oversight
2. **Graduated regulations:** Light touch for low-risk AI, intensive for high-risk
3. **International agreements:** Treaty framework for binding commitments
4. **Supporting processes:**
   - Scientific consensus building (what counts as dangerous?)
   - Standardization (safety protocols, evaluation methods)
   - Auditing (third-party verification)
   - Verification (prove compliance without revealing model weights)
   - Incentivization (carrots for cooperation, sticks for defection)

### 5.2 Verification Challenge

**Source:** arXiv:2504.12914 "In Which Areas of Technical AI Safety Could Geopolitical Rivals Cooperate?" (2025)

**Key Finding:** Verification mechanisms for AI systems are underdeveloped compared to nuclear arms control

**Technical Challenges:**
1. **No observable signature:** AI capabilities aren't detectable like radioactive isotopes
2. **Dual-use compute:** Same hardware used for safe/dangerous AI
3. **Software secrecy:** Model weights are proprietary (unlike nuclear materials which are physical)
4. **Rapid iteration:** AI models retrained frequently (unlike nuclear weapons which persist)

**Potential Cooperation Areas (Track-II dialogue, Western + Chinese academics):**
- Development of verification protocols
- Codified safety frameworks
- Standardization efforts (evaluation methods)
- Basic research on AI safety science (not applied capabilities)

**Geopolitical Reality:** US and China view AI as national security technology, limiting cooperation

**Simulation Parameter: Treaty Verification**
- **Verification difficulty:** 0.75 (high difficulty, scale 0-1 where 1 = impossible)
- **Defection detection probability:** 0.20-0.40 (low, unlike nuclear inspections ~0.90)
- **Cooperation prerequisite:** Mutual perception of catastrophic risk >50% (motivates transparency)

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM - Framework is theoretical (not yet implemented)

## 6. Geopolitical Constraints on Cooperation

### 6.1 US-China AI Competition

**Source:** CSIS "What the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance Reveals About Global Power Shifts" (2024), Paris AI Action Summit (2025)

**Tension Dynamics:**
- US views AI as strategic advantage (economic competitiveness, military edge)
- China views AI as path to technological self-sufficiency (avoid Western dependency)
- Both invest heavily in AI capabilities, less in safety
- Export controls limit technology sharing (US CHIPS Act, Export Control Reform Act)

**Paris AI Action Summit (2025):**
- US and UK declined to sign declaration on AI safety
- Cited concerns about constraining innovation competitiveness
- Highlights divergence between European (precautionary) and Anglo-American (innovation-first) approaches

**Simulation Implication:**
- Cooperation probability decreases with US-China tensions
- Regional blocs (EU, US/Five Eyes, China/BRICS) may form separate regimes
- Global coordination unlikely until post-catastrophe (reactive, not proactive)

**Simulation Parameter: Geopolitical Friction**
- **Cooperation penalty during high tension:** -0.30 (reduces baseline cooperation from 0.35 to 0.05)
- **Catastrophe threshold:** After AI-caused catastrophe (>10M deaths or >$1T damages), cooperation probability increases to 0.70 ("never let a crisis go to waste")

**Confidence Level:** HIGH - Well-documented geopolitical dynamics

### 6.2 Global North-South Divide

**Source:** UN Global Digital Compact consultations, ECDPM analysis

**Equity Concerns:**
- AI development concentrated in Global North (US, UK, EU, China)
- Global South excluded from governance discussions despite being affected
- Digital infrastructure gaps (compute, data, expertise) limit participation
- Risk of "AI colonialism" - Northern frameworks imposed on South

**Global Digital Compact Response:**
- Emphasis on digital public infrastructure investment
- Capacity building for Global South
- Multi-stakeholder consultations (not just governments)

**Challenge:** Resource constraints limit Global South influence (no seats at AISI Network table)

**Simulation Parameter: Inclusivity**
- **Global South participation:** 0.25 (low, improving slowly)
- **Impact on cooperation legitimacy:** Low participation reduces treaty compliance (seen as illegitimate)

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Well-documented but hard to quantify impact

## 7. Interoperable Frameworks vs. Global Standards

### 7.1 Regulatory Fragmentation

**Source:** TechPolicy.Press "A Proposed Scheme for International Diplomacy on AI Governance" (2024), Public Technology "The global AI safety divide" (2025)

**Current Reality:** Multiple competing frameworks
- **EU AI Act:** Risk-based, comprehensive regulation (operational 2025-2027)
- **US approach:** Sector-specific, light-touch (NIST AI RMF, executive orders)
- **UK approach:** Pro-innovation, context-specific
- **China approach:** State-controlled, social stability focus

**Expert Consensus:** Uniform global standards unlikely
- Different cultural values (privacy, free speech, state authority)
- Different risk tolerances (EU precautionary vs US innovation-first)
- Different enforcement capabilities (EU has strong regulators, Global South does not)

### 7.2 Interoperability Over Harmonization

**Proposed Path Forward:**
- Accept regulatory diversity as inevitable
- Focus on interoperability: Different national rules that can interface
- **Mutual recognition:** Countries accept each other's safety certifications (like car safety standards)
- **Baseline standards:** Minimal common requirements (transparency, redress mechanisms)
- **Information sharing:** Exchange about risks, incidents, best practices

**Analogy:** Internet protocols (TCP/IP) - different countries use different applications, but underlying protocols enable communication

**Simulation Parameter: Regulatory Convergence**
- **Harmonization probability:** 0.10 (very low, unlikely in next decade)
- **Interoperability probability:** 0.50 (moderate, achievable with effort)
- **Baseline safety floor:** Emerging through "regime complex" even without single treaty

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM - Expert opinion, not empirical data

## 8. Simulation Modeling Recommendations

### 8.1 Government Cooperation Mechanics

**Baseline Cooperation Probability:**
- **Pre-catastrophe:** 0.35 (moderate-low, current state)
- **Post-catastrophe (>10M deaths):** 0.70 (high, "Chernobyl moment")
- **Technical cooperation only:** 0.60 (easier than regulatory harmonization)

**Modifiers:**
- **Geopolitical tension (US-China, US-Russia):** -0.25 to -0.40
- **Shared catastrophic risk perception:** +0.20 to +0.40
- **Economic constraints:** -0.10 (safety investment competes with growth)
- **Domestic political pressure:** +0.15 (public demands action post-incident)

**Cooperation Domains (ranked by tractability):**
1. **Basic safety research:** 0.70 (non-rivalrous, mutual benefit)
2. **Evaluation methods:** 0.60 (standardization benefits all)
3. **Incident reporting:** 0.50 (transparency vs. competitive advantage tension)
4. **Compute limits:** 0.30 (highly rivalrous, hard to verify)
5. **Capability restrictions:** 0.20 (directly limits national power)

### 8.2 Treaty Effectiveness Parameters

**Adoption Dynamics:**
- **Time to negotiate:** 2-5 years (Council of Europe AI Convention took ~3 years)
- **Ratification lag:** 1-3 years (domestic legal process)
- **Implementation lag:** 2-4 years (build enforcement capacity)
- **Total time (recognition of need → effective enforcement):** 5-12 years

**Coverage:**
- **Western democracies:** 0.80 adoption probability
- **Authoritarian regimes:** 0.20 adoption probability (unless catastrophe forces cooperation)
- **Global coverage:** ~40-60% of AI development (by compute/investment)

**Compliance:**
- **Verification difficulty:** 0.75 (high, scale 0-1)
- **Defection detection:** 0.20-0.40 (low compared to nuclear inspections)
- **Enforcement:** Weak (sanctions, reputation costs, not military intervention)

**Effectiveness:**
- **Best case (strong cooperation):** 0.70 risk reduction (if all major powers cooperate with verification)
- **Realistic case (fragmented):** 0.30-0.40 risk reduction (regime complex provides some constraints)
- **Worst case (defection spiral):** 0.10 risk reduction (treaties become empty signaling)

### 8.3 Dynamic Factors

**Cooperation Deterioration:**
- Arms race dynamics: Each year of intense competition reduces cooperation by -0.05
- Espionage incidents: -0.15 cooperation penalty (trust erosion)
- Capability surprise: -0.20 cooperation penalty (if one country achieves unexpected breakthrough, others panic)

**Cooperation Recovery:**
- Near-miss events: +0.10 cooperation (wake-up call without catastrophe)
- Scientific consensus: +0.05 per year (as AI safety science matures)
- Leadership change: +0.15 (if pro-cooperation leaders elected in major powers)

## 9. Research Gaps and Uncertainties

### 9.1 Unknown Factors

1. **AI capability timelines:** When will AI reach dangerous capability thresholds? (Vast uncertainty, 2-30 year range)
2. **Catastrophe threshold:** How bad must an AI incident be to trigger cooperation? (Nuclear: Chernobyl ~0 deaths, massive response. AI: ?)
3. **Verification breakthroughs:** Could cryptographic proof systems enable verification without revealing model weights?
4. **Emergent cooperation:** Could non-state actors (labs, civil society) coordinate despite government failure?

### 9.2 Optimistic Scenarios

- AI safety science matures rapidly, verification becomes tractable
- Near-miss event (AI causes serious but sub-catastrophic harm) galvanizes cooperation before catastrophe
- Economic incentives align (AI safety becomes profitable through liability, insurance requirements)

### 9.3 Pessimistic Scenarios

- Arms race accelerates, cooperation collapses
- AI causes catastrophe before governance mechanisms mature
- Regulatory fragmentation creates "race to the bottom" (labs relocate to least regulated jurisdictions)
- Verification remains impossible, treaties become empty signaling
