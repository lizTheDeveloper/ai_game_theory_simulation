## Executive Summary

Marine Ice Sheet Instability (MICI) and Marine Ice Cliff Instability (MICI) represent mechanisms through which ice sheets grounded below sea level can undergo rapid, self-reinforcing collapse. The latest 2024-2025 research significantly revises earlier catastrophic projections downward for the 21st century, while confirming that temperature thresholds as low as 1.0-1.5°C above pre-industrial levels can trigger irreversible long-term collapse over centuries to millennia. For simulation purposes: WAIS tipping threshold = 1.0-1.5°C, Greenland threshold = 0.8-1.5°C, with collapse timescales ranging from decades (rapid pulses) to centuries (full deglaciation).

## 1. MICI Mechanisms

### 1.1 Original Hypothesis (DeConto & Pollard 2016)

DeConto and Pollard's groundbreaking 2016 Nature paper introduced the marine ice cliff instability (MICI) hypothesis, combining two previously underappreciated processes:

1. **Hydrofracturing of ice shelves**: Atmospheric warming causes surface meltwater to penetrate crevasses, widening them until ice shelves collapse
2. **Structural collapse of marine-terminating ice cliffs**: When ice cliffs exceed ~90 meters in height, they become structurally unstable and collapse under their own weight, exposing progressively thicker ice in a self-reinforcing chain reaction

**Key finding:** Antarctica could contribute >1 meter of sea level rise by 2100 and >15 meters by 2500 under high-emission scenarios (RCP8.5).

**Sources:**
- [DeConto, R.M. & Pollard, D. (2016). Contribution of Antarctica to past and future sea-level rise. Nature 531, 591-597](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17145)
- [Carbon Brief: Studies shed new light on Antarctica's future contribution to sea level rise](https://www.carbonbrief.org/studies-shed-new-light-on-antarcticas-future-contribution-to-sea-level-rise/)

### 1.2 Revised Understanding (2021-2025)

**Major revision (DeConto, Pollard, Alley et al. 2021):**
After incorporating improved model physics and revised atmospheric forcing, DeConto and colleagues substantially reduced their 2100 projections:

- **2016 projection:** 64-105 cm from Antarctica by 2100 (RCP8.5)
- **2021 projection:** 34 cm median by 2100 (RCP8.5)
- **Reason:** Improved physics delayed onset of surface melt by ~25 years

**Long-term projections remain significant:**
- 1 meter contribution by 2125
- 9.6 meters by 2300 under RCP8.5 (almost 10x more than +1.5°C scenarios)

**Sources:**
- [DeConto, R.M., Pollard, D., Alley, R.B. et al. (2021). The Paris Climate Agreement and future sea-level rise from Antarctica. Nature 593, 83-89](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33953408/)

**Critical 2024 reassessment (Morlighem et al.):**
The most important recent finding comes from Morlighem et al. (2024) in Science Advances, which challenges MICI vulnerability for the 21st century:

- **Three independent ice sheet models** simulating Amundsen Sea Embayment (including Thwaites Glacier) all showed NO further retreat in the 21st century after hypothetical ice shelf collapse
- **Mechanism:** When forced to retreat into deeper basins with taller cliffs, rapid thinning and velocity increases actually REDUCED calving rates, stabilizing the cliff
- **Conclusion:** "Thwaites may be less vulnerable to MICI than previously thought, and the most extreme projections of ice loss from the West Antarctic Ice Sheet are less likely"

**Sources:**
- [Morlighem, M. et al. (2024). The West Antarctic Ice Sheet may not be vulnerable to marine ice cliff instability during the 21st century. Science Advances 10(33)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ado7794)

### 1.3 Historical Evidence

**Geological confirmation of MICI mechanism:**
- Iceberg-keel plough marks in Pine Island Bay indicate calving-margin thicknesses consistent with MICI structural collapse thresholds
- Rapid MICI-driven retreat occurred ~12,300 years ago and terminated before ~11,200 years ago (~1,100-year event)
- Pine Island Glacier underwent 50-year period of MISI (Marine Ice Sheet Instability) ending in the 1990s when it reached new stable position

**Recent dramatic examples:**
- **Larsen A collapse (1995):** Ice shelf collapsed, glaciers behind immediately accelerated
- **Larsen B collapse (2002):** 3,250 km² ice shelf collapsed over 3 weeks in February 2002, introducing "completely new timescale to glaciology"

**Sources:**
- [Nature: Evidence of marine ice-cliff instability in Pine Island Bay from iceberg-keel plough marks](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24458)
- [Antarctic Glaciers: Marine Ice Sheet instability](https://www.antarcticglaciers.org/antarctica-2/west-antarctic-ice-sheet-2/marine-ice-sheets/)

## 2. Temperature Thresholds & Probabilities

### 2.1 West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS)

**Critical thresholds:**
- **Tipping point range:** 1.0-2.0°C above pre-industrial
- **Most likely threshold:** 1.0-1.5°C above pre-industrial
- **Current status:** At +1.2°C (2025), we are WITHIN or APPROACHING the threshold range

**Evidence:**
- Armstrong McKay et al. (2022): WAIS collapse becomes "possible" from 1.0°C warming, "likely" below 2.0°C
- Recent studies suggest threshold may be as low as 1.0°C
- 2024 research found that even holding climate constant at present-day values, Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers still collapse, indicating "today's mass loss rates are a precursor of deglaciation"

**Preservation requirements:**
- May require sustained cooling to 1°C BELOW pre-industrial (i.e., -0.2°C relative to 2025)
- Or 2°C below 2020 temperatures

**Sources:**
- [Armstrong McKay, D.I. et al. (2022). Exceeding 1.5°C global warming could trigger multiple climate tipping points. Science 377, eabn7950](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abn7950)
- [Nature Climate Change: Warming of +1.5°C is too high for polar ice sheets](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-025-02299-w)
- [Carbon Brief: How close is the West Antarctic ice sheet to a 'tipping point'?](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-how-close-is-the-west-antarctic-ice-sheet-to-a-tipping-point/)

### 2.2 Greenland Ice Sheet (GIS)

**Critical thresholds:**
- **Tipping point range:** 0.8-3.0°C above pre-industrial
- **Current status:** Already in "possible" range (current warming ~1.2°C)

**Evidence:**
- Armstrong McKay et al. (2022): Greenland collapse becomes "possible" from 0.8°C warming
- Multiple studies suggest tipping point between 0.8-3.0°C, with best estimates around 1.5°C
- Collapse would unfold over centuries to millennia but becomes IRREVERSIBLE beyond threshold

**Reversibility:**
- Recent research (2023) shows that abrupt melting following temperature overshoot CAN be mitigated if cooling returns below 1.5°C
- However, sustained warming beyond threshold leads to irreversible commitment

**Sources:**
- [Armstrong McKay et al. (2022) - see above]
- [Nature: Overshooting the critical threshold for the Greenland ice sheet](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06503-9)

### 2.3 East Antarctic Ice Sheet (EAIS)

**Critical thresholds:**
- **Marine basin collapse:** 2-4°C above pre-industrial
- **Full EAIS melt:** >8°C above pre-industrial

**Note:** Less relevant for near-term simulation (pre-2100) given current +1.2°C warming.

**Sources:**
- [Armstrong McKay et al. (2022) - see above]

## 3. Magnitudes of Sea Level Rise

### 3.1 Total Potential Contributions

**West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS):**
- Full collapse: **3.3-5 meters** of global sea level rise
- Thwaites Glacier alone: ~0.6 meters, but acts as "cork" holding back larger WAIS collapse

**Greenland Ice Sheet:**
- Full collapse: **7.4 meters** of global sea level rise

**East Antarctic Ice Sheet:**
- Marine basins: **3-5 meters**
- Full sheet: **53 meters** (very long timescale, >8°C threshold)

**Sources:**
- [IPCC AR6 WG1 Chapter 9: Ocean, Cryosphere and Sea Level Change](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter_09.pdf)

### 3.2 Century-Scale Projections (by 2100)

**Under high emissions (RCP8.5 / SSP5-8.5):**
- Antarctica total: **34 cm median** (DeConto et al. 2021) to **up to 1 meter** (older high-MICI scenarios)
- Greenland: **5-33 cm** (Aschwanden et al. 2019)
- Combined: **39-130+ cm** from ice sheets alone (plus thermal expansion, mountain glaciers)

**Under Paris Agreement (+1.5°C stabilization):**
- Antarctica: **<10 cm by 2100**, but **~1 meter by 2300**
- Greenland: **5-15 cm by 2100**

**Sources:**
- [DeConto et al. (2021) - see above]
- [Aschwanden, A. et al. (2019). Contribution of the Greenland Ice Sheet to sea level over the next millennium. Science Advances 5(6)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aav9396)

### 3.3 Abrupt Pulse Events

**Mechanism:** Sudden collapse of ice shelves or marine-terminating sections can produce discrete sea level rise pulses.

**Historical example:**
- Meltwater Pulse 1A (~14,600 years ago): **14-18 meters** over ~340 years (~4 cm/year average)
- Likely included contributions from multiple ice sheets

**Simulation parameters for "abrupt" events:**
- **Magnitude per event:** 0.5-3.0 meters (single ice sheet sector collapse)
- **Duration:** 50-200 years (rapid phase of individual collapse)
- **Frequency:** Multiple pulses possible if multiple sectors destabilize

**Note:** 2024 research suggests 21st century abrupt pulses are LESS likely than previously thought for WAIS, but remain possible under extreme warming or cascading failures.

**Sources:**
- [Historical meltwater pulse research - referenced in geological studies]

## 4. Timescales of Collapse

### 4.1 Ice Shelf Collapse (Initial Trigger)

**Timescale:** Weeks to years
**Example:** Larsen B collapsed over **3 weeks** in February 2002

**Mechanism:** Hydrofracturing from surface melt weakens ice shelf structure until catastrophic failure.

### 4.2 Glacier Acceleration (Immediate Response)

**Timescale:** Years to decades
**Mechanism:** Loss of buttressing ice shelf causes immediate speedup of glaciers feeding into ocean

**Current observations:**
- Thwaites Glacier retreat has **doubled in speed over past 30 years**
- Currently losing **~50 billion tonnes of ice annually**
- Contributing **4% of global sea level rise**
- Flow volume into sea **doubled from 1990s to 2010s**

**Sources:**
- [ScienceDaily: Satellites spot rapid "Doomsday Glacier" collapse](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/11/251130205511.htm)
- [Scientific American: Antarctica's 'Doomsday Glacier' Is Melting Even Faster](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/antarcticas-doomsday-glacier-is-melting-even-faster-than-scientists-thought/)
- [NOCO2: 2024 Update on Thwaites Glacier Collapse](https://noco2.com.au/blog/2024-latest-research-on-thwaites-glacier-collapse/)

### 4.3 Marine Ice Sheet Instability Phase

**Timescale:** Decades to centuries

**Mechanism:** Self-reinforcing retreat as grounding line moves into deeper basins, exposing taller cliffs and increasing calving rates.

**Key research findings:**
- **Decadal stabilization:** Viscoelastic mantle response can stabilize MISI over **decades to centuries** (low-viscosity mantle regions)
- **Centennial retreat:** Models show slow grounding-line retreat over **several centuries**, followed by rapid mass loss phase over **~200 years** at **~3 mm/year** contribution to global sea level
- **Pine Island historical example:** 50-year MISI period ending in 1990s

**Sources:**
- [Geophysical Research Letters: Rapid Viscoelastic Deformation Slows Marine Ice Sheet Instability](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019GL086446)
- [Antarctic Glaciers: Marine Ice Sheet instability](https://www.antarcticglaciers.org/antarctica-2/west-antarctic-ice-sheet-2/marine-ice-sheets/)

### 4.4 Full Ice Sheet Deglaciation

**Timescale:** Centuries to millennia

**West Antarctic Ice Sheet:**
- Fast estimate: **500 years** (under sustained high emissions)
- Moderate estimate: **2,000-5,000 years**
- Slow estimate: **up to 13,000 years**

**Greenland Ice Sheet:**
- **Centuries to millennia** depending on warming magnitude
- Once threshold crossed, collapse becomes irreversible even if cooling occurs (unless cooling is rapid and deep)

**Sources:**
- [Wikipedia: Ice sheet - Marine ice sheet instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_ice_sheet_instability)

### 4.5 Summary Timescales for Simulation

| Phase | Timescale | Sea Level Contribution |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| Ice shelf collapse | Weeks to years | Negligible (already floating) |
| Glacier acceleration | Years to decades | mm to cm per year |
| Rapid MISI phase | 50-200 years | 0.5-3 meters (per sector) |
| Full deglaciation | 500-13,000 years | 3-7+ meters (per ice sheet) |

**Thwaites Glacier (highest priority):**

- **2024-2025 status:** Eastern ice shelf "very near breakup" (within 2-3 years)

- Second-largest contributor in Amundsen Sea Embayment

**Amundsen Sea Embayment (collective):**
- Contains majority of marine-based WAIS

## 7. Integration with Existing Systems

### 7.1 Relationship to Temperature

**Direct relationship:**
- WAIS threshold: 1.0-1.5°C above pre-industrial
- GIS threshold: 0.8-1.5°C above pre-industrial
- Both thresholds ALREADY APPROACHED at current +1.2°C

**Simulation implementation:**
- Check global temperature against thresholds each year
- Once crossed, begin irreversible collapse trajectory (even if temperature later decreases)
- Rate of collapse depends on sustained warming magnitude and duration

### 7.2 Ocean Warming Dependencies

**Critical mechanism:** Ocean warming (not just atmospheric) drives marine ice sheet instability

**Southern Ocean:**
- Absorbed **35-43% of total ocean heat** (1970-2017)
- Drives Antarctic ice loss "overwhelmingly"
- Warming weakens ice shelves from below via basal melt

**AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation) interactions:**
- **Greenland meltwater → AMOC slowdown** → subsurface cooling in North Atlantic
- **Antarctic meltwater → Antarctic Bottom Water slowdown** → subsurface warming around Antarctica
- Complex feedback: WAIS collapse may partially STABILIZE AMOC (counterintuitive)

**Simulation integration:**
- Track ocean heat content separately from atmospheric temperature
- Ocean warming affects ice shelf basal melt rates
- Ice sheet meltwater feeds back to AMOC strength

**Sources:**
- [AGU: AMOC Stabilization Under the Interaction With Tipping Polar Ice Sheets](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022GL100305)
- [Nature Communications: Antarctic meltwater reduces AMOC through oceanic freshwater transport](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01670-7)
- [Science Advances: Meltwater from WAIS tipping affects AMOC resilience](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adw3852)

### 7.3 Albedo Feedback

**Mechanism:**
- Ice reflects 80-90% of solar radiation (high albedo)
- Open ocean absorbs 90% of solar radiation (low albedo)
- Ice loss → reduced albedo → more warming → more ice loss (positive feedback)

**Magnitude:**
- Ice loss in Arctic and Antarctic (1992-2018): warming impact equivalent to **10% of all greenhouse gas emissions** over that period

**Simulation integration:**
- Track ice sheet area/volume
- Calculate albedo change from ice loss
- Feed back into radiative forcing / temperature calculations

**Sources:**
- [Wikipedia: Ice-albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice–albedo_feedback)
- [PMC: Global warming due to loss of large ice masses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7591863/)

### 7.4 Existing Tipping Point Interactions

**Cascading risk (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022):**
- **Four tipping elements** become "likely" beyond 1.5°C:
  1. Greenland Ice Sheet collapse
  2. West Antarctic Ice Sheet collapse
  3. Abrupt permafrost thaw
  4. Warm-water coral reef die-off

**Cross-element interactions:**
- GIS collapse → AMOC slowdown → potential stabilization of WAIS (complex)
- Ice sheet collapse → albedo feedback → accelerated warming → triggers additional tipping points
- Permafrost thaw → methane/CO2 release → accelerated warming → faster ice sheet collapse

**Simulation implications:**
- Model ice sheet tipping as interconnected with other tipping elements
- Track cumulative warming from feedbacks
- Implement cascading probability increases

## 8. Uncertainties and Limitations

### 8.1 Major Scientific Uncertainties

**MICI mechanism validity:**
- ❌ **2016 consensus:** MICI is major threat, could cause >1m rise by 2100
- ✅ **2024 consensus:** MICI unlikely to cause catastrophic 21st century collapse, but long-term risk remains
- ⚠️ **Remaining uncertainty:** Role in geological past events, potential under extreme warming (>3°C)

**Model physics gaps:**
- Ice-ocean interface processes poorly understood
- Sub-shelf circulation and basal melt rates highly uncertain
- Fracture mechanics and calving parameterizations simplified

**Timescale uncertainty:**
- Fast collapse scenarios: 500 years
- Slow collapse scenarios: 13,000 years
- **Difference factor:** 26x spread in estimates

**Sources:**
- [Morlighem et al. (2024) - see above]
- [Reviews of Geophysics: Interactions and Feedbacks Between Ice Sheets and Earth System](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2018RG000600)

### 8.2 Regional vs. Global Sea Level

**Important distinction:**
- Ice sheet collapse does NOT produce uniform global sea level rise
- Regional variations due to:
  - Gravitational effects (ice mass attracts ocean water; when ice melts, local sea level FALLS near ice sheet, rises more elsewhere)
  - Glacial isostatic adjustment (land rebounds after ice mass removed)
  - Ocean circulation changes

**Example:** WAIS collapse → sea level rise 20-30% HIGHER than global average in Northern Hemisphere

**Simulation simplification:**
- Use global mean sea level rise for global impacts
- Apply regional multipliers for coastal impact calculations

### 8.3 Abrupt vs. Gradual Scenarios

**Scenario uncertainty:**
- **Gradual:** Ice sheet contribution stays <50 cm by 2100, then accelerates (currently favored by 2024 research)
- **Abrupt:** Multiple rapid pulses totaling 1-3 meters by 2100 (lower probability post-2024)

**Simulation approach:**
- Base case: Gradual scenario aligned with DeConto et al. 2021
- Low-probability events: Abrupt pulse scenarios triggered by extreme warming (>3°C) or cascading failures
- Use probabilistic approach: P(abrupt pulse | temperature, ocean warming, prior collapse)

### 8.4 Knowledge Gaps Requiring Assumptions

**For simulation parameter setting, assume:**

1. **Threshold temperatures:** WAIS = 1.0-1.5°C, GIS = 0.8-1.5°C (use middle of range if deterministic)
2. **21st century contribution:** 30-80 cm combined (all sources, high emissions)
3. **Abrupt pulse probability:** <10% for 21st century (unless warming >3°C)
4. **Collapse timescale:** 200-2000 years for full deglaciation (use 500-1000 years for base case)
5. **Population displacement:** ~100 million people per meter sea level rise
6. **Economic damages:** Scale super-linearly (~quadratically) with sea level rise magnitude

**Flag for sensitivity analysis:**
- MICI mechanism strength (off / weak / moderate / strong)
- Threshold temperature (±0.3°C variation)
- Collapse timescale (200-2000 year range)
- Abrupt pulse probability (1-20% range)

```typescript
function checkIceSheetTipping(state: GameState, rng: () => number): void {
  const temp = state.globalTemp;  // °C above pre-industrial

  // Check WAIS threshold
  if (state.iceSheets.waisStable && temp >= THRESHOLDS.WAIS_TIPPING_MIN) {
    const exceedance = temp - THRESHOLDS.WAIS_TIPPING_MEDIAN;
    const probability = sigmoid(exceedance, k=2);  // Steep S-curve

    if (rng() < probability) {
      state.iceSheets.waisStable = false;
      state.iceSheets.waisYearsCrossed = 0;
      logEvent("🌊❄️ WAIS TIPPING POINT CROSSED", { temp });

      // Begin irreversible collapse trajectory
      initiateWAISCollapse(state);
    }
  }

  // Check GIS threshold (similar logic)
  if (state.iceSheets.gisStable && temp >= THRESHOLDS.GIS_TIPPING_MIN) {
    // Similar probability calculation
    initiateGISCollapse(state);
  }

  // Check for abrupt pulse events (low probability)
  if (!state.iceSheets.waisStable && temp > 2.5) {
    const pulseProbability = COLLAPSE_RATES.ABRUPT_PULSE_PROBABILITY_BASE *
                            (temp - 2.0);  // Increases with warming
    if (rng() < pulseProbability / 120) {  // Per month probability
      triggerAbruptPulse(state, rng);
    }
  }
}

function updateSeaLevelRise(state: GameState, deltaMonths: number): void {
  if (!state.iceSheets.waisStable) {
    // Determine phase of collapse
    const years = state.iceSheets.waisYearsCrossed;
    let rate: number;

    if (years < COLLAPSE_RATES.GLACIER_ACCELERATION_TIME) {
      rate = COLLAPSE_RATES.INITIAL_ACCELERATION;
    } else if (years < COLLAPSE_RATES.RAPID_MISI_DURATION) {
      rate = COLLAPSE_RATES.RAPID_MISI_PHASE;
    } else {
      rate = COLLAPSE_RATES.FULL_DEGLACIATION;
    }

    // Cap at maximum contribution
    const monthlyRise = rate * (deltaMonths / 12);
    const newTotal = Math.min(
      state.iceSheets.cumulativeSeaLevelRise + monthlyRise,
      THRESHOLDS.WAIS_MAX_CONTRIBUTION
    );

    state.iceSheets.cumulativeSeaLevelRise = newTotal;
  }

  // Similar logic for GIS

  // Update coastal impacts
  updateCoastalImpacts(state);
}

function updateCoastalImpacts(state: GameState): void {
  const slr = state.iceSheets.cumulativeSeaLevelRise;

  // Population displacement (linear)
  state.iceSheets.coastalPopulationDisplaced =
    slr * COASTAL_IMPACTS.DISPLACED_PER_METER;

  // Infrastructure damage (quadratic)
  state.iceSheets.coastalInfrastructureDamage =
    COASTAL_IMPACTS.INFRASTRUCTURE_DAMAGE_LINEAR * slr +
    COASTAL_IMPACTS.INFRASTRUCTURE_DAMAGE_QUADRATIC * slr * slr;

  // Agricultural land loss (interpolated from vulnerability range)
  const fraction = interpolate(
    slr, 0, 5,  // 0-5 meters
    COASTAL_IMPACTS.AG_LAND_VULNERABLE_LOW,
    COASTAL_IMPACTS.AG_LAND_VULNERABLE_HIGH
  );
  state.iceSheets.agriculturalLandLost =
    state.globalAgLand * fraction;

  // Feed back to food system
  state.foodSecurity.globalYield *= (1 - fraction * 0.5);  // 50% productivity loss on lost land
}
```
