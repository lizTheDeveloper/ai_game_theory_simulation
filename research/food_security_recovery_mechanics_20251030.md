## Executive Summary

Agricultural recovery from major shocks (climate, conflict, nuclear) follows complex, context-dependent patterns. Key findings:

1. **Recovery timelines vary dramatically by shock type:** Nuclear winter (7-15 years), post-WWII Europe (2-5 years), Green Revolution improvements (5-15 years for widespread adoption)
2. **Recovery is rarely exponential:** Most evidence suggests logistic/S-curve patterns with initial lag, acceleration phase, then plateau at 80-95% of pre-shock productivity
3. **System interdependencies are critical:** Food security recovery enables recovery of other systems (health, economy, governance), but also depends on them
4. **Thresholds matter more than averages:** Minimum viable conditions (temperature, precipitation, infrastructure, labor) determine whether recovery can begin
5. **Regional variation is massive:** Tropical agriculture shows different resilience patterns than temperate zones

## 1. Nuclear Winter Recovery (Highest Severity Shock)

### Key Sources

**Xia et al. 2022** - "Global food insecurity and famine from reduced crop, marine fishery and livestock production due to climate disruption from nuclear war soot injection"
- **Publication:** *Nature Food*, Volume 3, No. 8, pp. 586-596 (2022)
- **DOI:** Available at https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-022-00573-0
- **Authors:** L. Xia, A. Robock, K. Scherrer, C. Harrison, B.L. Bodirsky, I. Weindl, J. Jägermeyr, C. Bardeen, O.B. Toon, R. Heneghan
- **Citations:** 847+ (Google Scholar)
- **Credibility:** HIGH - Peer-reviewed in top-tier Nature journal, NASA GISS authors, widely cited

**Quantitative Parameters:**

**150 Tg Soot Injection Scenario (US-Russia nuclear war):**
- **Temperature drop:** -9°C global average, -25°C Northern Hemisphere midlatitudes (Year 1-2)
- **Duration of nuclear winter:** 2-5 years (temperatures below freezing in summer)
- **Precipitation reduction:** 68% by end of Year 2
- **Solar radiation reduction:** 85% by end of Year 2
- **Crop production reduction:** 89% global reduction
- **Recovery timeline:** 7-15 years to return to pre-shock levels
- **Human mortality:** 75% global fatality rate from starvation (5+ billion deaths)

**5 Tg Soot Injection Scenario (India-Pakistan regional war):**
- **Crop production decline:** 7% annual maize production reduction
- **Recovery timeline:** 7-10 years
- **Human mortality:** 2+ billion deaths from starvation

**Recovery Pattern:**
- **Years 0-2:** Virtually no agricultural production possible in mid-latitude regions (nuclear winter conditions)
- **Years 2-5:** Gradual warming begins, but UV-B damage peaks at 6-8 years post-war (7% additional maize reduction)
- **Years 5-10:** Major crops (maize, soybean, rice, spring wheat, cotton, sugarcane) show >70% reduction in global production
- **Years 10-15:** Gradual return to control levels, likely asymptoting at 90-95% of pre-war productivity

**Geographic Variation:**
- **Only viable regions (Years 0-5):** Tropical zones (India, Central Africa, South America)
- **Impossible for 2+ years:** Traditional midlatitude agricultural regions (US Corn Belt, Europe, China)
- **Permanent changes:** Some regions may never fully recover due to soil degradation, species extinction

**Critical Quote from Xia et al. 2022:**
> "By the end of the second year, temperature would reduce by 16°C, solar radiation by 85%, and precipitation by 68%... Soot injections larger than 5 Tg would lead to mass food shortages, and livestock and aquatic food production would be unable to compensate for reduced crop output, in almost all countries."

## 2. Post-WWII Agricultural Recovery (High Severity, Infrastructure Destruction)

### Key Sources

**Post-war reconstruction literature (multiple sources)**
- **Publication:** *European Review of Economic History*, *CEPR VoxEU*, Wikipedia sources on Marshall Plan
- **Credibility:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Historical economic analysis, but not peer-reviewed agricultural research

**Quantitative Parameters:**

**Immediate Post-War (1945-1947):**
- **Agricultural production (1947):** 83% of 1938 pre-war levels (Europe-wide average)
- **Geographic variation:** UK, Netherlands, France restored to pre-war levels by end of 1947 (faster recovery)
- **Recovery timeline:** 2-5 years to reach pre-war productivity

**Marshall Plan Period (1948-1952):**
- **Financial aid:** $12 billion over 4 years ($13.2 billion in total program)
- **Recovery acceleration:** Fastest growth period in European history
- **Industrial production increase:** 35% (1948-1952)
- **Agricultural production:** "Substantially surpassing pre-war levels" by 1952
- **Recovery timeline:** 5-7 years to exceed pre-war productivity

**Recovery Mechanisms:**
1. **Infrastructure investment:** Transport system renewal, industrial/agricultural equipment modernization
2. **Policy continuity:** Maintained wartime agricultural stimulus policies, deepened intervention
3. **International coordination:** European Economic Community (1957), Common Agricultural Policy
4. **Capital injection:** Marshall Plan provided critical funding for reconstruction

**Recovery Pattern:**
- **Phase 1 (1945-1947):** Rapid initial recovery to 80-85% baseline (2 years)
- **Phase 2 (1948-1952):** Acceleration to 100-110% baseline (5 years total)
- **Phase 3 (1952+):** Continued growth beyond pre-war levels

**Critical Insight:** Post-WWII recovery was FASTER than nuclear winter projections (2-7 years vs 7-15 years) because:
1. Climate conditions remained stable (no temperature/precipitation disruption)
2. Seed stocks and genetic diversity preserved
3. Labor force survived (though depleted)
4. International aid available (Marshall Plan)
5. Institutional knowledge intact

**Relevance Score: HIGH** - Demonstrates that with climate stability and international support, recovery can reach 100% within 5-7 years. Without these conditions, expect 2-3× longer timelines.

## 3. Green Revolution Agricultural Improvements (Technology-Driven Recovery)

### Key Sources

**Multiple peer-reviewed sources** - *PNAS*, *Journal of Political Economy*, Frontiers, PMC, Wikipedia
- **Credibility:** HIGH - Multiple peer-reviewed sources, well-documented historical case

**Quantitative Parameters:**

**Timeline:**
- **1943:** Mexican Agricultural Program begins (wheat focus)
- **1966:** IR8 "miracle rice" becomes available
- **1965-2010:** High-yielding varieties (HYVs) increase yields by 44%
- **Peak adoption:** 1960s-1990s (30-year transformation)

**Yield Improvements:**
- **Cereal production:** Tripled with only 30% increase in land area
- **Rice/wheat in Asia:** Yields doubled (1960s-1990s)
- **IR8 rice:** 5 tons/hectare (no fertilizer), 10 tons/hectare (optimal conditions)
- **Global cereal yields:** ~3× production per unit land area (1961 vs today)
- **Maize in semi-arid Ethiopia:** 25% yield increase from conservation agriculture techniques (2024 trials)

**Food Security Impact:**
- **India wheat production:** 12 million tons (1965) → 20 million tons (1970) - 67% increase in 5 years
- **India self-sufficiency:** Achieved 1971 (6 years from Green Revolution start)
- **Caloric availability:** +33% average Asian consumption, poverty rate cut by 50%
- **Lives saved:** Norman Borlaug credited with saving 1+ billion people from starvation

**Economic Impact:**
- **10-year delay cost:** 17% of GDP per capita (2010 estimate), +223 million people in developing world

**Recovery Pattern (Adoption S-Curve):**
- **Phase 1 (1943-1965):** Research and development, initial trials (22 years)
- **Phase 2 (1966-1975):** Rapid adoption, 5-10 years to reach 50% penetration in adopting regions
- **Phase 3 (1975-1995):** Continued diffusion, plateau at ~80-90% adoption
- **Phase 4 (1995-present):** Incremental improvements, second Green Revolution in Africa (2004-2022)

**Africa Green Revolution (2004-2022):**
- **Cereal production increase:** 92% over 18 years
- **Yield improvements:** 41% of increase (vs 37% from area expansion)
- **Comparison to Asia:** Africa's 92% increase over 18 years comparable to Asia's 85% increase over equivalent period (1966-1984)

**Relevance Score: HIGH** - Demonstrates that technological interventions can produce 2-3× yield improvements within 5-10 years of adoption, but require:
1. Functioning seed distribution systems
2. Fertilizer availability
3. Irrigation infrastructure
4. Farmer education/extension services
5. Market access for surplus production

**Critical Quote:**
> "Without the Green Revolution, caloric availability would have declined by around 11-13%"

## 4. Climate Threshold Parameters for Agricultural Viability

### Key Sources

**Climate change impacts literature (2024-2025)**
- **Publications:** *Scientific Reports* (Nature), PMC, USDA Climate Adaptation Plan 2024, EPA reports
- **Credibility:** HIGH - Peer-reviewed, government reports from authoritative sources

**Temperature Thresholds:**

**Crop-Specific Sensitivities:**
- **Wheat:** Daily temperatures above 9°C cause yield declines
- **Maize, rice, soybeans:** Sensitive above 29-31°C
- **Maize (specific):** 5% yield loss for each day shifting from 25°C to 40°C
- **General pattern:** Yields increase incrementally up to threshold, then drop dramatically beyond it

**Critical Temperature Ranges:**
- **Nuclear winter:** -15 to -25°C below baseline (agriculture impossible for 2-5 years)
- **Heat stress:** +2-4°C above baseline reduces yields 10-25% for major crops
- **Optimal range:** Crop-specific, but generally 15-30°C for temperate crops, 20-35°C for tropical crops

**Precipitation Thresholds:**

**Minimum Viable Precipitation:**
- **Wheat:** 614 mm base precipitation (lowest among major crops)
- **General pattern:** 1% increase in precipitation → 0.53% yield increase
- **Drought conditions:** Yield losses of 21% (wheat), 40% (maize), 50% (rice)

**Precipitation Variability:**
- **Historical range:** -677% to +1470% in wheat-growing areas (extreme outliers)
- **2024-2025 droughts:** Global impacts across Africa, Asia, Americas with multi-year persistence

**Recovery Requirement:**
- **Minimum:** Return to 80% of historical precipitation baseline for agricultural restart
- **Optimal:** Return to 100% historical baseline for full productivity recovery

**Future Projections (2024-2050):**
- **Maize:** -8.1% global yield reduction (highest among major crops)
- **Wheat:** -6.2% global yield reduction
- **Rice:** -3.5% global yield reduction
- **Sub-Saharan Africa:** -10 to -20% staple crop yields by 2050
- **South Asia:** -10 to -15% rice/wheat production by mid-century
- **Ethiopia (specific):** -15% maize yields from temperature increase + erratic rainfall

**Relevance Score: HIGH** - Critical for simulation recovery mechanics. Agriculture cannot begin recovering until:
1. Temperature returns to within 5°C of baseline (absolute minimum, 2°C preferred)
2. Precipitation returns to 60%+ of baseline (80%+ for full recovery)
3. Growing season length sufficient (120+ days frost-free for major crops)

## 5. Resilience Metrics and Recovery Dynamics

### Key Sources

**Agricultural resilience literature (2024)**
- **Publications:** *Agronomy for Sustainable Development* (2024), OECD reports, PMC, Agricultural and Food Economics
- **Credibility:** HIGH - Recent peer-reviewed research, international organization reports

**Quantitative Resilience Metrics:**

**Five Core Metrics Identified:**
1. **Average values** (baseline productivity)
2. **Probability of exceeding critical threshold** (failure risk)
3. **Variability** (year-to-year stability)
4. **Recovery time** (time to return to baseline after shock)
5. **Trend over time** (long-term trajectory)

**Recovery Time Findings:**
- **Acknowledged as key indicator** but only used in 2 studies (major research gap)
- **Most farms perform well in only 2 of 4 resilience capacities** (OECD finding)
- **Threshold-based approach:** Resilience measured by "reciprocal of squared coefficient of variance, proportional to return period of largest shocks system can absorb"

**OECD Resilience Framework (2024):**
- **Definition:** Balance between preparation, absorption, adaptation, and transformation
- **Farm-level assessment:** Most farms strong in only 2/4 capacities
- **Recovery phase:** Critical but under-measured

**Minimum Viable Metrics Recommendations:**
- Start with small set of clearly defined, actionable, meaningful, generally accepted metrics
- Define thresholds based on several measurement periods (not absolute upfront values)
- Recovery time = critical gap in current measurement practices

**Relevance Score: MEDIUM-HIGH** - Provides framework for thinking about recovery, but lacks specific quantitative parameters for implementation. Confirms that recovery time is understudied, validating need for this research synthesis.

## 7. Soil Regeneration Timescales

### Key Sources

**Soil science literature**
- **Publications:** Wikipedia (well-cited), SARE, Nature Education, PMC, USDA reports
- **Credibility:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Mixed sources, but core findings consistent across sources

**Quantitative Parameters:**

**Global Soil Degradation:**
- **Carbon loss from agriculture:** 133 billion tonnes carbon lost since agriculture began (most in last 200 years with mechanization)
- **Current soil carbon storage:** 3,012 billion tonnes (top 2 meters) - more than atmosphere (879 Bt) + vegetation (600 Bt) combined
- **Degraded agricultural land:** 40% of all agricultural land (20 million km²) degraded by at least one process
- **Global degradation extent:** 30% of ice-free land (39.3 million km²) moderately to highly degraded
- **2050 projection:** 90% of Earth's soils could be degraded by 2050 without protective measures

**Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) Levels:**
- **Optimal SOC (tropical):** 1.0-1.5% in root zone
- **Optimal SOC (temperate):** 1.5-2.0% in root zone
- **Degraded soils (smallholders):** As low as 0.1% SOC in root zone (500-600 million farmers affected)

**Regeneration Timescales:**
- **Rapidly evolving soils (A horizon only):** Tens to hundreds of years
- **Fully developed soils:** Thousands of years to restore
- **Deep soils (B/C horizons):** More than 1 million years for slowly developing soils
- **Practical restoration:** "Restoration techniques will never totally succeed to restore a soil (and fauna/flora) that took more than 1,000 years to build up"

**Successful Case Study:**
- **Quesungual Slash and Mulch Agroforestry System (Honduras):**
  - **Area:** 60,000 hectares secondary forest regeneration
  - **Beneficiaries:** 6,000+ local families
  - **Impacts:** Reduced erosion, improved crop yields and quality of life
  - **Timeline:** Not specified, but implies multi-year to decade-scale intervention

**Relevance Score: MEDIUM** - Critical for understanding PERMANENT degradation. Key insight:
1. Surface soil (top 20-30 cm) can recover in 10-50 years with active intervention
2. Deep soil structure takes 100-1,000+ years to recover
3. Complete restoration to pre-degradation state may be impossible
4. Agricultural productivity can return to 70-90% within 10-20 years even without full soil recovery

**Simulation Implication:** Recovery can asymptote at 80-90% of pre-shock productivity if soil degradation is severe. Don't assume 100% recovery in all scenarios.

## 9. Recovery Pattern Synthesis: Logistic vs Exponential

### Theoretical Framework

**Logistic Growth (S-Curve) Model:**
- **Equation:** dP/dt = rP(1 - P/K), where P = productivity, r = recovery rate, K = carrying capacity
- **Phases:**
  1. **Initiation/lag (0-20% recovery):** Slow initial growth, infrastructure/seed stock restoration
  2. **Acceleration (20-80% recovery):** Rapid growth as systems come online
  3. **Deceleration (80-95% recovery):** Diminishing returns, resource constraints
  4. **Saturation (95-100% recovery):** Plateau at new equilibrium (may be below original baseline)

**Evidence for Logistic Pattern:**
- **Post-WWII recovery:** 83% by 1947 (2 years), 100%+ by 1952 (7 years) - clear S-curve
- **Green Revolution adoption:** Slow initial uptake (1943-1966), rapid acceleration (1966-1980), plateau (1980-2000)
- **Nuclear winter projections:** 0-5% recovery for 2-5 years (lag), then gradual acceleration over 5-10 years
- **Biological basis:** Plant growth, soil regeneration, population recovery all follow logistic patterns

**Exponential Pattern (Rare in Agriculture):**
- **Pure exponential recovery:** dP/dt = rP (unbounded growth)
- **Limited applicability:** Only seen in very early recovery phases when resources unlimited
- **Example:** Initial 1945-1947 European recovery (~40% gain in 2 years) before resource constraints emerged

**Recommended Model for Simulation:**

**Logistic recovery with shock-dependent parameters:**

```
Recovery_t = K / (1 + ((K - P0) / P0) * exp(-r * (t - t_lag)))

Where:
- K = asymptotic productivity (70-100% of pre-shock, depends on soil/climate damage)
- P0 = productivity immediately after shock (0-20% depending on severity)
- r = recovery rate (0.1-0.5 per year, depends on aid/infrastructure/climate)
- t_lag = lag before recovery begins (0-5 years, depends on climate stabilization)
- t = time since shock
```

**Parameter Ranges by Shock Type:**

| Shock Type | P0 (initial) | K (asymptote) | r (rate/year) | t_lag (years) |
|------------|--------------|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| Nuclear winter (150 Tg) | 0-5% | 70-90% | 0.1-0.2 | 2-5 years |
| Regional nuclear (5 Tg) | 10-30% | 80-95% | 0.15-0.3 | 0-2 years |
| Climate shock (severe drought) | 30-60% | 90-100% | 0.2-0.4 | 0-1 year |
| Post-conflict (infrastructure destroyed) | 20-50% | 85-95% | 0.1-0.25 | 1-3 years |
| Technology improvement (Green Revolution) | 100% | 150-300% | 0.15-0.3 | 5-15 years (adoption lag) |

**Relevance Score: CRITICAL** - This synthesis provides implementation-ready parameters for simulation recovery mechanics.

## 10. System Interdependencies and Cascading Recovery

### Key Sources

**Systems resilience literature (2024)**
- **Publications:** *Discover Food* (Springer, 2025), CSIS reports, Council on Strategic Risks, World Bank, FAO State of Food Security 2024
- **Credibility:** HIGH - Recent peer-reviewed research, international organizations

**Key Findings:**

**Food-Water Interdependencies:**
- **Correlation:** "The less water-secure a household is, the more likely it is to also experience food insecurity"
- **System relationship:** Food and freshwater constitute interdependent systems (not just correlated)
- **Implication:** Food security recovery requires water security recovery first (or simultaneously)

**Cascading Effects Framework:**
- **2015 simulation finding:** "Designed to help decision-makers understand interdependencies of food, climate, trade and political stability, and cascading effects of collective and individual policy decisions"
- **2024 finding:** "Multiple drivers of food insecurity and malnutrition, often with cascading effects, conflate to create exceptional hurdles to achieving SDGs by 2030"
- **Supply chain insight:** "Global food supply chain is complex, interconnected system—changes in single part can have cascading effects throughout entire network"

**Food Security as Enabler:**
Food security recovery enables:
1. **Health system recovery:** Adequate nutrition required for disease resistance, medical treatment effectiveness
2. **Economic recovery:** Agricultural productivity drives rural economies, creates surplus for urban populations
3. **Political stability:** Food insecurity is primary driver of conflict, migration, regime collapse
4. **Education system recovery:** Malnourished children cannot learn effectively

**Food Security Dependencies:**
Food security recovery depends on:
1. **Water security:** Irrigation, drinking water, food processing
2. **Energy security:** Mechanization, irrigation pumps, food processing/storage
3. **Climate stability:** Temperature, precipitation, growing season length
4. **Institutional capacity:** Government extension services, market regulation, trade policy
5. **Labor force:** Sufficient population with agricultural skills
6. **Infrastructure:** Roads, storage facilities, markets
7. **Seed/genetics:** Preserved crop diversity and adapted varieties
8. **Capital:** Investment in equipment, inputs, land improvement

**Recovery Threshold Insights:**
- **Minimum viable food security:** ~1,800 kcal/person/day (below this, cascading health failures)
- **Minimum for system recovery:** ~2,200 kcal/person/day (enough surplus energy for labor, economic activity)
- **Minimum for stability:** ~2,500 kcal/person/day (UN baseline, enables full functioning)

**Cascading Recovery Sequence (Hypothesis from Literature):**
1. **Phase 1 (Immediate post-shock, 0-2 years):** Survival food (stored reserves, emergency aid, wild food)
2. **Phase 2 (Early recovery, 2-5 years):** Subsistence agriculture resumes, food security reaches 1,800-2,000 kcal/person/day
3. **Phase 3 (Acceleration, 5-10 years):** Surplus production begins, food security reaches 2,200-2,500 kcal/person/day, enabling economic recovery
4. **Phase 4 (Stabilization, 10-15 years):** Full food security (2,500+ kcal), agricultural sector drives broader economic growth

**Relevance Score: CRITICAL** - Essential for understanding why food security recovery is both critical path item and bottlenecked by other systems. Simulation must model bidirectional dependencies.

## 11. Regional Variation: Tropical vs Temperate Agriculture

### Key Sources

**Climate-specific agricultural literature**
- **Publications:** PMC, FAO reports, OECD, various 2024 peer-reviewed sources
- **Credibility:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Consistent findings across sources

**Drought Impact by Region:**

**Tropical Agriculture:**
- **Yield losses:** 50% rice reduction (most vulnerable major crop)
- **Drought recovery mechanisms:** Improved water management "led to greater resilience to moderate drought, helping avoid crop losses"
- **2024 trial results:** 25% maize yield increase in semi-arid Ethiopian regions from conservation agriculture
- **Climate change projection:** "Many regions of cultivated land may become unsuitable, while other tropical regions may produce more crops" (mixed impacts)
- **Nuclear winter resilience:** Only viable production regions for first 2-5 years after 150 Tg injection (India, Central Africa, South America)

**Temperate Agriculture:**
- **Yield losses:** 21% wheat reduction (more resilient than tropical rice)
- **Drought challenges:** "Unpredictable weather patterns and pests" as primary concerns
- **Climate change projection:** 10-15% yield reductions projected for major temperate crops by mid-century
- **Nuclear winter vulnerability:** Completely impossible for 2-5 years (summer temperatures below freezing)
- **Post-WWII recovery advantage:** Europe (temperate) recovered faster than tropical post-colonial regions

**Comparative Resilience:**
- **Tropical advantages:** Year-round growing season, multiple harvests possible, nuclear winter refuge
- **Tropical disadvantages:** Higher disease/pest pressure, soil erosion vulnerability, more vulnerable to heat stress
- **Temperate advantages:** Lower disease pressure, better soil organic matter retention, established infrastructure
- **Temperate disadvantages:** Single annual harvest, vulnerable to nuclear winter, climate change pushing growing zones poleward

**Recovery Rate Implications:**
- **Tropical regions:** Faster recovery from moderate shocks (multiple growing seasons), but more vulnerable to extreme heat
- **Temperate regions:** Slower recovery (single annual harvest), but more resilient infrastructure and institutions
- **Nuclear winter scenario:** Tropical regions = only viable option for 2-5 years, but with severely degraded productivity (50-80% loss)

**Relevance Score: HIGH** - Regional differentiation is critical for realistic simulation. Recovery mechanics should vary by climate zone.

## 12. Research Gaps and Uncertainties

### Critical Gaps Identified:

1. **Recovery time metrics:** Acknowledged as critical but only measured in 2 studies globally (OECD 2024 finding)

2. **Post-nuclear winter agriculture:** Shi et al. 2025 contradicts Xia et al. 2022 on regional impacts. Requires reconciliation or sensitivity analysis

3. **Soil regeneration timescales:** Wide uncertainty (10-1,000+ years depending on degradation severity). Limited quantitative data on productivity recovery despite partial soil recovery

4. **Cascading recovery sequences:** Strong theoretical framework but limited empirical validation. No peer-reviewed studies quantifying food security → economic recovery → political stability feedback loops

5. **Threshold effects:** Many studies report thresholds exist but don't quantify them precisely. Temperature thresholds vary by study (9°C for wheat vs 29-31°C for other crops - need crop-specific models)

6. **Adaptation effectiveness:** Green Revolution shows technology can improve yields 2-3×, but unclear if these gains persist under severe climate stress. Limited data on HYV performance under nuclear winter conditions

7. **Conflict vs climate recovery:** Only 6 peer-reviewed studies on agricultural interventions in conflict settings (1980-2022 search). Massive research gap despite practical importance

### Recommended Assumptions for Simulation:

Given uncertainties, recommend these conservative assumptions:

1. **Base recovery on logistic model** with parameters from nuclear winter literature (most severe, best documented)

2. **Regional differentiation:** Tropical = 1.5× faster recovery rate, but 0.8× asymptotic productivity compared to temperate

3. **Soil degradation penalty:** If shock duration >5 years, reduce asymptotic productivity by 10-20% (permanent degradation)

4. **Threshold gates:** Agriculture cannot begin recovering until temperature within 5°C and precipitation >60% of baseline

5. **Cascading dependencies:** Food security recovery rate = min(food_recovery_rate, 0.5×water_recovery_rate + 0.3×infrastructure_recovery_rate + 0.2×institutional_recovery_rate)

6. **Conflict penalty:** If conflict active, multiply recovery rate by 0.5× (post-conflict slower than post-disaster)

7. **Technology multiplier:** If agricultural R&D functional, increase asymptotic productivity by 10-30% over 10-20 year period (Green Revolution analogy)

```typescript
  yearsPostShock: number,
  shockSeverity: 'catastrophic' | 'severe' | 'moderate',
  region: 'tropical' | 'temperate',
  climateStabilized: boolean,
  conflictActive: boolean,
  infrastructureIntact: number  // 0-1 scale
): number {

  // Parameters by shock severity
  const params = {
    catastrophic: { P0: 0.02, K: 0.80, r: 0.15, lag: 4 },  // Nuclear winter 150 Tg
    severe: { P0: 0.20, K: 0.90, r: 0.20, lag: 2 },        // Regional nuclear 5 Tg
    moderate: { P0: 0.50, K: 0.95, r: 0.30, lag: 0.5 }     // Severe drought
  };

  const { P0, K, r, lag } = params[shockSeverity];

  // Regional multipliers
  const regionalMultiplier = region === 'tropical' ? 1.5 : 1.0;
  const regionalAsymptote = region === 'tropical' ? 0.8 : 1.0;

  // Climate stabilization gate
  if (!climateStabilized && yearsPostShock < lag) {
    return P0;  // No recovery until climate stabilizes
  }

  // Effective time accounting for lag
  const effectiveTime = Math.max(0, yearsPostShock - lag);

  // Logistic recovery calculation
  const adjustedK = K * regionalAsymptote;
  const adjustedR = r * regionalMultiplier;
  const logisticRecovery = adjustedK / (1 + ((adjustedK - P0) / P0) * Math.exp(-adjustedR * effectiveTime));

  // Conflict penalty
  const conflictPenalty = conflictActive ? 0.5 : 1.0;

  // Infrastructure dependency (recovery rate scales with infrastructure)
  const infrastructureMultiplier = 0.3 + 0.7 * infrastructureIntact;

  return logisticRecovery * conflictPenalty * infrastructureMultiplier;
}
```


```typescript
function calculateCascadingRecoveryRate(
  foodRecoveryRate: number,
  waterSecurityLevel: number,  // 0-1
  infrastructureLevel: number,  // 0-1
  institutionalCapacity: number  // 0-1

  // Food security recovery is gated by dependencies
  const waterGate = 0.5 * waterSecurityLevel;

  const institutionGate = 0.2 * institutionalCapacity;
```
