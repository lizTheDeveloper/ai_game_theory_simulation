## Executive Summary

**Major Finding:** Research published in Global Change Biology (2025) confirms that **ocean acidification crossed its planetary boundary around 2020**, making it the **7th of 9 planetary boundaries breached**. This finding revises the 2023 assessment which showed oceans at 19% reduction (just below the 20% threshold).

**Key Quantitative Findings:**
- **40% of global surface ocean** has crossed the 20% aragonite reduction threshold (2020)
- **60% of subsurface water (100-200m depth)** has crossed threshold
- **All 7 ocean regions** have entered boundary uncertainty zones
- **4 of 7 regions** (Arctic, North Pacific, Southern Ocean, North Atlantic) have definitively crossed
- **Arctic hit hardest:** 26% reduction in aragonite saturation, 78% of region crossed threshold

**Critical Context:** The ocean acidification boundary was first crossed in the **1980s** if using the more protective 10% reduction threshold recommended by researchers. The 2020 crossing represents a 5-year lag in detection due to data processing timelines.

**Implications for Simulation:**
- Ocean acidification is no longer a "future threat" - it's a current catastrophe
- Marine ecosystem collapse is accelerating (coral -43% habitat, pteropods +61% dissolution conditions)
- Planetary boundary framework now shows **7 of 9 boundaries crossed** (updated from 6 in 2023)

## 1. Planetary Boundary Definition

### 1.1 Aragonite Saturation State (ΩArag)

**What it measures:** The saturation level of aragonite (calcium carbonate) in seawater, critical for shell-building organisms.

**Pre-industrial baseline:** ΩArag = 3.51 ± 0.065 (range 3.44–3.57)

**Planetary boundary threshold:** 20% reduction from pre-industrial = **ΩArag 2.80 ± 0.05**

**Proposed stricter threshold:** 10% reduction = ΩArag 3.16 (crossed in 1980s)

**Citation:** Steffen, A., et al. (2025). "Ocean Acidification: Another Planetary Boundary Crossed." *Global Change Biology*, 31(6), e70238. https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.70238

**Why aragonite?** Aragonite is more soluble than calcite, making it a sensitive early warning indicator for ocean acidification impacts on marine calcifiers.

## 2. Current Global Status (2020 Data)

### 2.1 Global Average

**Surface ocean:** ΩArag = 2.90 ± 0.06
- **Reduction:** 17.3% ± 5.0% from pre-industrial
- **Status:** Within uncertainty range of 20% boundary (crossed when accounting for uncertainty)

**Key finding:** The global average masks severe regional impacts. While global average shows 17.3%, **40% of ocean surface area** has crossed the 20% threshold.

**Critical finding:** Polar regions (Arctic, Southern Ocean) are most severely impacted, with >80% of surface area crossed.

### 2.3 Depth Profile (Subsurface Acidification)

**Subsurface impacts are WORSE than surface:**

- **100m depth:** 58.3% ± 10.7% of global ocean area crossed 20% threshold
- **200m depth:** 61.0% ± 10.6% crossed threshold

**Why subsurface is worse:** Deeper waters have longer residence times and accumulated more anthropogenic CO₂ without surface mixing dilution.

## 3. Biological Thresholds and Marine Ecosystem Impacts

### 3.1 Species-Specific Critical Thresholds

**Warm-water corals:**
- **Marginal conditions:** ΩArag ≤ 3.5
- **Habitat loss (1750–2020):** 43% reduction in suitable habitat
- **Marginal area increase:** 30% more ocean area now marginal/unsuitable

**Pteropods (floating snails, critical to food web):**
- **Mild shell dissolution:** ΩArag ≤ 1.5
- **Severe dissolution:** ΩArag ≤ 1.2
- **Impact (polar regions, 0–200m):**
  - +61% increase in mild dissolution conditions
  - +16% increase in severe dissolution conditions

**Bivalves (oysters, clams):**
- **Marginal conditions:** ΩArag ≤ 1.8
- **Impact (coastal regions, 0–25m):** +12% increase in marginal conditions

**Food web implications:** Pteropods are critical prey for salmon, whales, and seabirds. Their decline cascades through marine food webs.

### 3.2 Ecosystem Tipping Points

**Coral reef collapse threshold:** When ΩArag drops below 3.5, reef-building coral communities cannot sustain net calcification (dissolution exceeds growth).

**Current coral status:**
- 43% of low-latitude coral habitat lost (1750–2020)
- Under 10% boundary (stricter), coral loss would be limited to 28%
- Under 20% boundary (current), coral loss projected to reach 60%+ this century

## 4. Historical Timeline

### 4.1 When Thresholds Were Crossed

**1980s:** First crossed **10% reduction boundary** (ΩArag 3.16)
- Arctic surface undersaturation began increasing
- Coastal oyster habitat degradation began accelerating

**2000:** Entire surface ocean crossed 10% boundary

**2020:** Crossed **20% reduction boundary** in 40% of surface ocean
- All 7 regions entered uncertainty zone
- 4 regions definitively crossed

**2025:** Boundary crossing confirmed through peer-reviewed analysis

**Detection lag:** The crossing occurred in 2020, but wasn't confirmed until 2025 due to:
- Data collection and processing timelines
- Statistical uncertainty calculations
- Peer review process

### 4.2 Rate of Change

**Surface pH drop since industrial revolution:** 0.1 pH units
- **Acidity increase:** ~30% (pH is logarithmic scale)
- **ΩArag decline:** 17.3% global average, 26% in Arctic

**Acceleration:** The rate of acidification is increasing due to:
1. Rising atmospheric CO₂ (currently 422 ppm, up from 280 ppm pre-industrial)
2. Ocean warming reducing CO₂ buffering capacity
3. Arctic amplification effects (polar waters absorb more CO₂ at low temps)

## 5. Revised Boundary Recommendation

### 5.1 From 20% to 10% Reduction

**Authors' recommendation:** The planetary boundary should be **10% reduction** (ΩArag 3.16), not 20%.

**Rationale:**
1. **Arctic protection:** 10% limit restricts Arctic undersaturation to <10% of region (vs. 78% under 20% limit)
2. **Coral habitat:** 10% limit caps coral habitat loss at 28% (vs. 43% already lost under 20% limit)
3. **Coastal aquaculture:** 10% limit restricts unsuitable oyster habitat to 10% (vs. current levels)

**Policy implication:** If the stricter 10% boundary is adopted, Earth crossed it **40+ years ago (1980s)**, not in 2020.

### 5.2 Why the 20% Boundary Was Initially Chosen

**Original reasoning (2009 Planetary Boundaries framework):**
- Based on coral calcification threshold (ΩArag ~3.5 for warm-water corals)
- 20% reduction was thought to leave safety margin
- Assumed regional variability would be less severe

**What changed:**
- Arctic acidification worse than expected (26% reduction vs. 17% global)
- Subsurface acidification exceeds surface (61% of 200m depth crossed)
- Pteropod dissolution thresholds reached faster than predicted

#### Coral Resilience Under Combined Stressors (Jury et al. 2024)

**Citation:** Jury CP, et al. (2024). Experimental coral reef communities transform yet persist under mitigated future ocean warming and acidification. *Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A*, 121(45):e2407112121.

**Experimental conditions (2-year study):**
- **+2°C warming** + **-0.2 pH units** (acidification) combined
- Aligns with Paris Agreement mitigation targets

**Key findings:**
- Coral communities **persist as transformed ecosystems** rather than collapsing entirely
- **Biodiversity maintained** despite structural changes
- **Calcification reduced** but not eliminated
- Communities shift to novel species compositions ("no-analog ecosystems")

**Simulation implication:** Coral reefs may not "disappear" but transform into degraded states with reduced function:
- Lower biodiversity
- Reduced calcification rates (50–75% of baseline)
- Altered species composition (loss of branching corals, persistence of massive corals)
- Maintained ecosystem presence but reduced ecosystem services

## 7. Cross-System Interactions

### 7.1 Ocean Acidification + Warming

**Combined stressor amplification:**
- Thermal stress reduces coral calcification rates
- Acidification reduces carbonate availability
- **Combined effect:** Multiplicative, not additive (1.5-2× mortality increase)

**Citation:** Hughes, T. P., et al. (2018). "Global warming transforms coral reef assemblages." *Nature*, 556(7702), 492-496.

### 7.2 Ocean Acidification + Overfishing

**Food web cascade:**
1. Acidification kills pteropods (zooplankton)
2. Salmon, whales lose critical food source
3. Overfishing removes top predators, preventing ecosystem adaptation
4. Jellyfish blooms replace fish in acidified waters

### 7.3 Ocean Acidification + Novel Entities (Plastics)

**Microplastic-acidification synergy:**
- Microplastics reduce filtering capacity of bivalves
- Acidification reduces shell-building capacity
- Combined: 40-60% higher mortality in polluted acidified waters

## 9. Policy and Mitigation Implications

### 9.1 Irreversibility Timescale

**Ocean acidification is SLOW to reverse:**
- Atmospheric CO₂ removal doesn't immediately restore ocean pH
- Ocean mixing time: 500-1,000 years for deep ocean turnover
- ΩArag recovery lag: 100-300 years after atmospheric CO₂ stabilizes

**Critical finding:** Even if we achieve net-zero emissions by 2050, ocean acidification will continue worsening until ~2070-2100 due to ocean thermal inertia.

### 9.2 Remaining Carbon Budget

**To stay below 10% boundary (already crossed):** Negative emissions required

**To prevent 30% reduction (catastrophic threshold):**
- **Remaining budget:** ~150-200 GtCO₂ (at current emissions, ~4-5 years)
- **Required by:** 2030 peak emissions, 2050 net-zero

### 9.3 Marine Protection Interventions

**Local interventions (limited effectiveness):**
- Kelp/seagrass restoration (localized pH buffering)
- Alkalinity enhancement (experimental, unproven at scale)
- Marine protected areas (reduce stressors, don't fix pH)

**Only solution:** Rapid decarbonization to limit atmospheric CO₂
